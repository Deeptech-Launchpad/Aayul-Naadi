01 — Identity

The brand: Aayu, and a copilot called Nadi

Aayu (आयु) means lifespan — not a moment of health, but the whole length of it. That is exactly the claim the product makes: your health is a continuous line, and care today only ever samples it. Nadi (नाड़ी) is the pulse — and in the older diagnostic tradition, the practice of reading a person's whole state from one continuous signal. So the two names carry the product thesis without a word of explanation: Aayu is the record of your life; Nadi is what reads it.

Wordmark

aayu.

Set in Bricolage Grotesque 800, lowercase, tight tracking, terminal period. The period is the mark of a reading taken — a full stop on a measurement. Never set in title case, never stretched, never on a gradient.

Symbol

Pulse into leaf

An ECG trace that resolves into a single sprouting leaf on its right end. Clinical instrument on the left, living thing on the right — the trace is the data, the leaf is the person. Works at 16 px as a favicon and 512 px as an app icon.

Voice

Precise, never alarming

Aayu states what it measured, when, and against which range. It never says "concerning" or "you should" — it says "above your usual range since June" and "worth raising at your next visit." Every claim is followed by where it came from.

Palette

Jade rather than the medical blue every health app defaults to — deep enough to read as clinical, warm enough to not read as a hospital. Turmeric is the only accent, and it is spent on one thing: something that needs your attention. Grounds are a cool neutral pulled slightly green so the interface reads as one material.

02 — Architecture of the experience

Five tabs, one record, everything cites its source

The whole product resolves to a single idea: there is one record, and every screen is a different lens on it. Ingest screens write to it, dashboard screens read from it, and Nadi reasons over it — never over a separate copy. That is why the tab bar has five items and not nine.

Tab 01

Today

The dashboard. What changed since you last looked, what is out of range, what needs an action today.

Tab 02

Record

Labs, vitals, documents, EHR history and activity — one continuous timeline you can filter and search.

Tab 03 · centre

Nadi

Ask anything. Answers are drawn from your record and every sentence carries a citation you can tap.

Tab 04

Care

Medications, screenings due, care gaps, appointments and the visit-prep report you take to your doctor.

Tab 05

Profile

What only you know — conditions, allergies, family history, lifestyle, goals — plus settings and security.

Cross-cutting

Add, from anywhere

A single "＋" on every tab opens the same capture sheet: log a vital, upload a document, take a photo of a lab report, add a medication.

03 — Screens · Group A

Getting in

Four screens stand between a stranger and your health record. They are deliberately the least clever screens in the product: no marketing, no illustration, no "skip for now" on anything that protects data. Recovery is designed before it is needed, because a lost passphrase on an end-to-end encrypted record is unrecoverable by anyone — including me.

03 — Screens · Group B

Setting up the record

Onboarding has one job: get enough into the record that the first Nadi answer is genuinely about you. It asks for consent before it asks for anything else, then takes five short steps — and every step can be finished later without blocking the app. The deck's "Health Profile" pillar is this flow plus its permanent home at F2.

03 — Screens · Group C

The daily loop

This is the part of the product you actually live in — thirty seconds a day, most days. The dashboard leads with what changed, not with a wall of numbers, because a value you have seen unchanged for a week is not information. Every tile is a doorway into its own trend, and every trend has an "Ask Nadi about this" that carries the metric into chat as context.

03 — Screens · Group D

Nadi — the part that has to be trustworthy

A health chatbot that answers confidently from nothing is worse than no chatbot. The design rule for Nadi is absolute: every factual sentence carries a citation to a row in your record, and a question the record cannot answer gets "I don't have that" instead of a guess. The grounding drawer exists so you can audit any answer in two taps, and the visit-prep report is what turns all of this into fifteen useful minutes with your doctor.

03 — Screens · Group E

Getting data in

Every health app dies at the ingestion step. Aayu's answer is that a photograph of a lab report is a first-class input — Claude reads the page, proposes structured markers with its confidence and the exact text it read them from, and you confirm before anything is written. Automatic where a connector exists, photographable where it does not, and never silently wrong.

03 — Screens · Group F

Managing the record

Medications, the profile that only you can supply, and the three security screens that decide whether any of this is safe to keep. The security surfaces are not buried in a settings list — they are a destination with their own home, because in a product holding a complete medical record they are a feature, not an obligation.

04 — Security model

Built for a record you would actually put your own labs into

This app holds a complete medical history for one person — you. That makes it a higher-value target than most consumer software and it deserves the controls below, all of which are in scope for the build rather than "phase two".

LayerWhat is implementedWhy it is there
IdentityArgon2id passphrase hashing (64 MB, t=3), mandatory TOTP second factor, ten single-use recovery codes, breached-password rejection at set time.Passphrase alone is one stolen credential away from your entire history.
SessionsOpaque server-side sessions in httpOnly · Secure · SameSite=Strict cookies, rotated on privilege change, 30-day absolute lifetime, per-device revocation, idle app-lock at 2 minutes.Stateless JWTs cannot be revoked; a stolen laptop should not mean a permanent session.
Encryption at restPer-user data key, AES-256-GCM on every health field and every uploaded file. The data key is wrapped by a key derived from your passphrase and by a KMS-held master key.A database dump on its own yields ciphertext. Column-level, not just disk-level.
Encryption in transitTLS 1.3 only, HSTS preload, strict CSP with nonces, Referrer-Policy: no-referrer, no third-party scripts or analytics on any authenticated page.Third-party tags are the most common way health data leaks from health apps.
Model boundaryDe-identification before every Claude call — name, email, address, phone, MRN and record IDs stripped and replaced with a coarse subject descriptor. Zero-retention API settings. No training on your data, ever.The model needs your values, not your identity, to be useful.
Prompt-injection defenceUploaded document text and clinical notes are wrapped as untrusted data with explicit instructions never to follow directives inside them; tool calls are allow-listed and scoped to your user id server-side; every tool result is schema-validated.A PDF is attacker-controlled input the moment someone else generated it.
AuthorisationEvery query carries the session's user id at the data-access layer — enforced in the query builder, not in route handlers — plus row-level policies in the database as a second, independent gate.The classic health-app breach is an ID in a URL that nobody checked.
AuditAppend-only log of every read and write of health data, written in the same transaction as the action, surfaced to you in full at F4.An audit log you cannot read is a log that protects the vendor, not you.
SharingSigned, expiring, single-purpose, revocable links; every open recorded. No public URLs, ever.Sharing is the only outbound path, so it is the one that must be airtight.
Rate & abusePer-account and per-IP limits on auth, uploads and model calls; exponential backoff and lockout; upload type/size validation with content sniffing; documents processed in an isolated worker.Also caps what a stolen session can cost you in API spend.
OperationalSecrets from environment only, never in the repository; no PHI in application logs or error reports; dependency and secret scanning in CI; encrypted, restorable backups.Most real incidents are operational, not cryptographic.
Stated plainly

What this is not

Aayu is a personal tool for one person's own record. It is not a HIPAA-covered entity, not a medical device, and not a diagnostic system. It does not dose, diagnose, or triage emergencies, and every screen that touches clinical judgement says so. If it ever holds someone else's data, a compliance programme — BAAs, formal risk assessment, breach process — comes before that, not after.

Design consequence

Deterministic decides, the model explains

Out-of-range flags, screening due-dates, interaction warnings and adherence maths are computed in code from a rules table. Claude reads your record, explains it, extracts from documents and answers questions — it never decides what counts as abnormal. This is what makes the answers reproducible and the mistakes findable.

05 — How it is built

One record, five tools, no magic

The technical shape follows from the design: a normalised observation store that every source writes into, a small set of typed tools that read from it, and Claude sitting on top of those tools with no direct database access at all.

Client

Next.js 15 · React · TypeScript

App Router, server components, installable as a PWA so it behaves like an app on your phone without an app-store round trip. Tailwind carrying the token system defined in section 01.

Data

Postgres · Prisma

One observations table for every measured value regardless of source, plus documents, medications, conditions, care rules, sessions and the audit log. Row-level security as the second authorisation gate.

Intelligence

Claude · Opus 5

Streaming chat with tool use for grounded answers; vision for document and device-display extraction; structured JSON output validated against Zod schemas before anything is written.

The five tools Nadi can call

Each is scoped to your user id on the server, filtered by your consent categories, and returns rows that become citations in the answer.

ToolArgumentsReturns
get_profile—Age, sex, conditions, allergies, active medications, goals, family history
get_labsmarkers[], from, to, limitLab results with value, unit, reference range, lab, collection date
get_seriesmetric, from, to, aggregateTime series with computed mean, trend and in-range percentage
get_medicationsactive, windowSchedule, dose history, adherence rate, refill projection
search_documentsquery, from, toMatching passages with document, page and extraction provenance

Where Claude is used, and where it is deliberately not

Model does this
  • Grounded chat — answers assembled from tool results, every claim cited
  • Document extraction — lab PDFs, photos, discharge notes, device displays → validated JSON with confidence and source text
  • The morning read — one daily synthesis of what changed and what it lines up with
  • Visit-prep drafting — turning an interval of the record into questions worth asking
  • Plain language — explaining a marker, a rule, or a trend at the reading level you ask for
Code does this
  • Range flags — reference intervals and condition-adjusted targets
  • Care gaps — a rules table with guideline source, population, interval and the query that satisfies it
  • Interactions & allergies — coded lookup on every medication change
  • Statistics — averages, trends, in-range percentages, adherence
  • Deduplication — the same result from two sources becomes one row with two provenances

Four decisions before the build starts

Everything above is specified enough to build from. These four change the shape of it, so they are yours to make.

  1. Brand. Aayu / Nadi as presented, or a different name and palette — this is the one thing that is expensive to change late.
  2. Where it runs. Hosted, so it is on your phone from anywhere, or local-only on a machine you own, so the record never leaves your house.
  3. Scope of the first build. All six stages, or stages 1–4 first so you have something in your hands sooner.
  4. Real data. Whether the first version should ingest your actual labs and records, or run on realistic sample data until you have decided you trust it.
